The three scrapers taught me that "scraping" isn't one skill — it's a different 
technique per site. Lakeshore was pure CSS selectors: a class selector for the 
cards, an id-free h2.menu-section__title heading, and the ~ sibling selector to 
reach the div.menu-grid that followed it without ever naming the grid directly. 
That's the part that didn't click until I tried it by hand in the Console tab 
first — document.querySelectorAll let me see immediately that ~ div.menu-grid 
found the right element, and ~ * would have grabbed the description paragraph 
above it instead. Hilltop was different again: pd.read_html handed me every
already parsed, and the hard part was realizing the heading-to-table pairing has 
to go through zip(), since neither element knows about the other — the page just 
happens to put them in the same order. melt collapsing the S/M/L columns into rows 
was the one piece of pandas I had to draw out on paper before it made sense.

Canal Street is where I actually got bitten. My first version of scrape_canal 
called query_selector_all("a.result-card") right after clicking "Show full menu" 
and got an empty list back — no error, just nothing. That's the lesson about a 
headless browser running JavaScript: the click returns before the fetch to 
menu.json resolves and the cards get drawn, so query_selector_all runs before 
the DOM exists. Adding wait_for_selector fixed the first load, but I still missed 
that "Show more" needed its own wait — wait_for_function comparing the card count 
before and after the click — because the same "page isn't done yet" problem happens 
on every page of pagination, not just the first one.


Part 2's fuzzy matching was conceptually the hardest. I expected a single score 
threshold to "solve" matching, but normalize_name only gets you normalized-string 
equality — it can't tell me that "Matcha Latte" (87 against "Mocha Latte") is a 
different drink, or that "PSL" (18 against "Pumpkin Spice Latte") is the same one. 
That's why the human-in-the-loop design exists: AUTO_MATCH and REVIEW aren't 
correctness thresholds, they're confidence thresholds, and the owner's saved 
decisions in item_matches.csv are what actually resolve the cases a score can't. 
The part I underestimated was that the "pool" of known names grows as match_items 
runs — Canal Street's "Matcha Latte" only auto-matches because Lakeshore's already 
got added to the list by the time Canal's row is processed. I had to trace through 
the loop by hand to see why order mattered.


For Part 3, gap_analysis's outer merge with indicator=True was new to me — I'd only 
ever used merge to combine columns, not to find out which rows came from which side. 
Forgetting to .astype(str) the _merge column before sorting cost me twenty minutes of 
a GitHub-issue-worthy debugging session, because its categorical dtype quietly sorts 
by the category's original order, not the labels I'd just renamed it to.


Where I need more practice: I can write a CSS selector when I can see the HTML, but 
I'm still slow at guessing the right selector blind — I'd benefit from more reps 
using just the Console's querySelectorAll before writing Python. I also want to get 
more comfortable with groupby().agg() using named aggregations (rivals="nunique", 
sold_by=lambda...) — I got it working by trial and error here, not from real 
understanding of when pandas allows the keyword form versus requiring tuples.
